Time-Resolved Impact Force Improves Neural Impact-Sound Generation

Companion page: result tables, controls, training details and audio examples.

Main comparison Protocol Ablations and controls Baselines Encoder robustness Signal metrics Audio Code and data
TL;DR. On 100 real objects with synchronously measured impact force (3,548 strikes), we train a DDSP (differentiable digital signal processing) generator and a mel-spectrogram conditional variational autoencoder (CVAE). Both are conditioned at four cumulative levels: identity only (L1), the peak force (L2), a six-number descriptor (L3) and the time-resolved force frames (L4). Each level keeps the inputs of the level below and adds one. Scored by object-level Fréchet Audio Distance (FAD) with VGGish embeddings, L4 is the only level that improves on L1 for both generators, on objects seen and unseen in training. The peak and the descriptor help only in specific settings. For DDSP, both are detected as better on unseen objects and neither on seen objects. For the CVAE, the descriptor is detected as better only on seen objects, where the peak is detected as worse. For DDSP, pooling the force from 0.67 ms to 2 ms removes L4's gain over the descriptor on seen objects.

2 · Statistical protocol and detectable difference

Comparisons use a paired bootstrap. Objects are resampled with replacement 2,000 times, and each resample gives the mean per-object FAD difference between two systems. The clips of an object stay together in every resample. The 95% confidence interval (CI) is the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile of these resampled means. The two-sided p is twice the smaller share of resampled means on either side of zero. Holm correction raises the p values within a family of comparisons to account for the number of comparisons in that family. Cohen's d is the mean per-object difference divided by its standard deviation across objects. The primary metric is object-level FAD (VGGish embeddings). Each level's score is the mean over five training runs, and each run's checkpoint is chosen on validation loss. A difference is detected when p < 0.05, the 95% CI excludes zero and |d| ≥ 0.2; otherwise it is not detected. In the first two tables of Section 1, p is Holm-corrected; the note under each other table says whether its p is corrected.

The design detects a difference of ≈7% of the L1 FAD at 70% power under the Holm-corrected criterion, estimated from five L1 runs (DDSP, seen objects, validation set). On the DDSP generator, the peak and the descriptor change FAD on seen objects by at most ≈2% of the L1 FAD, below 7%, and are not detected. For the CVAE, on seen objects the descriptor (5.7% of its L1 FAD) is detected as better and the peak (8.2%) as worse.

Alternative descriptors

L3's six numbers come from the full 6 s recording of the strike. They are the peak, its pulse width (full width at half maximum, FWHM), the bounce count and the max bounce ratio of the later bounces. The last two are the decay constant after the peak and the asymmetry of rise and fall. Two of them are unreliable on this data. The pulse width is two samples or fewer at 48 kHz for 51% of strikes, and the decay-constant fit is degenerate for 9%. The windowed descriptor therefore computes six numbers on the 200 ms force window with corrected definitions. The windowed three-number descriptor keeps its peak, contact duration and asymmetry, and the impulse is the integral of the force magnitude. The PCA-6 descriptor is the six leading principal components of the force window, fitted on training strikes (seen objects only).

On seen objects no alternative descriptor improves on L1, so the seen-object result for L3 does not depend on the choice of descriptor. The PCA-6 descriptor keeps 93% of the variance of the force curve and 91% of that of its peak-normalised shape (test strikes). It still scores worse than L1 (d = +0.32) and worse than L4 (d = +0.99). The windowed descriptor is detected as worse than L4 on both splits. Against L4, L3's difference on unseen objects is not detected (+0.064 [−0.016, +0.158]).

Controls on L4

L4′. L4′ keeps identity and the frames of L4 and drops the peak and the descriptor. It is a control outside the four levels. For DDSP, dropping them lowers FAD on unseen objects (d = −0.85), and the change on seen objects is not detected. For the CVAE, FAD rises on both splits: +0.217 [+0.153, +0.279] on seen and +0.311 [+0.187, +0.439] on unseen objects. The CVAE encodes the force window into one global 32-D code, so its L4′ result concerns that code.

Frames at 2 ms and 6.7 ms. For DDSP, L4 pools the 200 ms force window in blocks of 32 samples (0.67 ms) into 300 frames with two channels, the root-mean-square and the maximum absolute value. The synthesiser also updates every 32 samples, so at L4 each force frame spans one synthesis frame. These controls pool the same window in blocks of 96 or 320 samples instead. The window then has 100 or 30 frames, and each pooled value is held over 3 or 10 synthesis frames. Each channel is z-scored with statistics computed for that block size. Identity, peak and descriptor are unchanged, and the change applies in training and at test.

Training variants

Pretraining trains on synthetic noise bursts, then fine-tunes on the real data; the envelope loss adds an envelope ℓ1 term to the loss. Of the two training variants, pretraining gives the larger improvement over L4 and is detected as better on both splits. Against the five L4 runs the envelope loss is not detected on either split. L4 runs 1–3 share the envelope-loss seeds and average 1.551 on seen objects (1.40 over all five runs). Against these three runs, the envelope loss is detected as better on seen objects: −0.124 [−0.203, −0.050], d = −0.32, p <0.001.

Envelope loss. The envelope loss targets a ringing artefact. On unseen objects the generated decay tail comes out as sustained broadband noise instead of the recording's tonal modal decay (Section 7, Figure B, bottom). The multi-resolution short-time Fourier transform loss changes little with decay shape. Disabling the modal branch of the DDSP generator (the 32 damped sinusoids that carry the resonant tail) changes the best validation loss by about 1%, and the ringing artefact persists. The envelope ℓ1 term has weight 8.8, chosen from a validation sweep; both splits use this weight and the three seeds 42–44. Adding the term raises the validation loss on unseen objects by 6.7%. Two of the three seen-object runs and all three unseen-object runs have a lower validation FAD than the L4 run of the same seed. The tail-energy index is the mean root-mean-square envelope over the 100–200 ms tail of the 200 ms window, divided by the envelope peak. On unseen-object test clips the index is 0.09 for L4 and 0.10 with the envelope loss; real recordings give 0.11.

Pretraining. The synthetic corpus consists of random decaying noise bursts convolved with 2,000 simulated room impulse responses, with the burst as the force input. On the validation set, FAD after fine-tuning on real data is 19–24% (seen) and 17–28% (unseen) lower than for L4 of the same seed. 4 · Baselines (test set)

Test set. White noise gives the FAD of an uninformative output. Modal synthesis fits up to 48 damped sinusoids per training object to its recordings, with the open-source SAMPLE package and ridge least squares, and the measured force excites them. On unseen objects, which have no fitted sinusoids, it uses one set combined from the training objects of the same material. Retrieval, which the paper's Table 1 does not list, returns the training recording whose force window is closest to the test strike's in cosine similarity. On unseen objects it can only return another object's recording and still has lower FAD than every DDSP system below. FAD scores recorded audio well even for the wrong object, so this page uses it only to rank generated outputs against each other. Section 6 shows which acoustic properties the levels change. Modal synthesis scores worse than every learned DDSP level on seen objects; on unseen objects its difference from L4 is not detected.

6 · Signal-domain metrics (descriptive)

From L1 to L4 (DDSP, no significance tests), the ℓ1 distance between log-mel energy envelopes improves (seen 0.0595 → 0.0565, unseen 0.1046 → 0.0930). Per-clip log-spectral distance improves on unseen objects (11.65 → 10.12 dB) and is slightly worse on seen objects (6.98 → 7.13 dB). The "envelope shape" column below is a different measure, the envelope-shape distance of the paper's Table 3.

Per-clip descriptor differences

How to read these tables. For every test clip we compute standard audio descriptors on the real recording and on the generated version. Each cell is the absolute difference, averaged per object and then over objects, as mean ± s.d. across the five training runs (real vs real row: across ten random splits). All signals are peak-normalised per clip first, so no column depends on loudness. Lower means the generated clip is closer to the real one. Spectral centroid and bandwidth describe where the energy sits and how spread it is. Spectral flatness and contrast describe how tone-like or noise-like the spectrum is. The MFCC distance summarises overall timbre (coefficients 1–13, loudness coefficient excluded). Envelope shape is the ℓ1 distance between the peak-normalised loudness envelopes. T20 is the time for the envelope to fall 20 dB below its peak, and the column shows the difference in that decay time. The real vs real row is the floor. The object's real test strikes are split at random into two halves and paired one to one, over ten random splits. Differences at or below this row are within natural strike-to-strike variability.

DDSP generator. On seen objects the differences sit at or below the real-vs-real floor for most descriptors; the centroid and bandwidth differences grow from L1 to L4. On unseen objects every descriptor sits above the floor at L1. From L1 to L4, spectral centroid goes from 1,603 Hz to 1,156 Hz (floor 914), MFCC distance from 48.4 to 41.5 (floor 27.8). The decay-time difference goes from 39.3 to 32.7 ms (floor 32.6), and envelope shape reaches the floor at L4. Spectral flatness, the tone-versus-noise character of the sound, does not improve at any level. It is 4.41 dB at L1, 4.97 at L2, 4.60 at L3 and 4.40 at L4, against a floor of 2.54 dB.
